# Creative Studio

Prompts for generating and improving original work while preserving the creator's voice.

## 1. Generate concepts with useful creative distance

**Use when:** the obvious ideas all look alike.

```text
Act as a creative director helping me explore, not choose for me.

Project: [format and purpose]
Audience: [specific people]
Desired response: [what they should feel, understand, or do]
Constraints: [brand, medium, length, budget, taboo topics]
References I like and why: [list]
Ideas to avoid: [clichés, competitor territory, previous work]

Generate 12 concepts in four groups: safe, adjacent, surprising, and deliberately polarizing. For each give a one-line premise, the creative mechanism, and its main risk. Then combine two incompatible concepts into three hybrids.

Do not imitate a living artist's signature style. Abstract references into traits such as rhythm, contrast, density, framing, or emotional temperature. End with three questions that would help me select or improve a direction.
```

**Human check:** search for accidental similarity to existing campaigns or works before developing a concept.

## 2. Find and replace the clichés in a draft

**Use when:** a piece is competent but predictable.

```text
Review the draft below for clichés in language, structure, imagery, character, and argument.

Draft:
[paste]

Audience and purpose: [details]
Parts that must remain: [details]
Tone I want to preserve: [details]

Create a table with: excerpt, why it feels familiar, what function it serves, and three replacement strategies. Prefer changes that grow from details already present in the draft. Then rewrite only the three highest-impact passages, keeping my approximate sentence length and level of formality.

Do not erase deliberate genre conventions or rewrite the entire piece. Mark subjective judgments as opinions and explain them.
```

**Human check:** read the revisions aloud and reject any line that sounds polished but no longer sounds like you.

## 3. Turn a premise into a scene with pressure

**Use when:** you know what a story is about but the scene feels static.

```text
Help me design one scene from this premise: [premise].

Point-of-view character: [who]
What they want before the scene ends: [concrete want]
What they cannot say directly: [subtext]
Opposing force: [person, system, environment, self]
Known setting details: [details]
Story facts that cannot change: [facts]

Return:
1. The scene's change from opening state to closing state
2. Five escalating beats, each caused by the previous beat
3. Two physical details that can carry subtext
4. One choice that costs the character something
5. A 300–500 word exploratory draft

Avoid exposition that characters already know, generic banter, and a convenient resolution. Label any new story fact you invented so I can accept or reject it.
```

**Human check:** verify that the protagonist makes a meaningful choice rather than merely receiving information.

## 4. Critique a creative work without flattening its voice

**Use when:** you want actionable feedback, not a replacement draft.

```text
Critique this [story / script / visual brief / poem / concept]:
[paste]

My intent: [what I want it to do]
Target audience or venue: [details]
Stage of work: [exploration / draft / near final]
What kind of feedback I want now: [focus]
What is intentionally unconventional: [details]

Begin by describing what the work appears to be trying to do, without judging it. Then identify:
- two elements already supporting the intent
- three high-leverage gaps, with evidence from the work
- one reader response that may vary by audience
- the smallest revision experiment for each gap
- one strength that could be damaged by over-editing

Ask before producing a full rewrite. Distinguish craft observations from taste.
```

**Human check:** compare the critique with feedback from at least one real member of the intended audience.
